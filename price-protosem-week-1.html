How Recommendation Algorithms Work

The first session introduced recommendation algorithms—the intelligent systems behind suggestions such as “Customers also bought,” “Recommended for you,” and personalized content feeds on Spotify, Amazon, and YouTube.

We explored the two core approaches that power modern digital experiences:

Behavioral Clustering

Collaborative Filtering

Recommendations based on the preferences, ratings, and browsing behaviors of similar users.

Mechanism: If User A & User B share 90% common favorites, User B is recommended items User A loved.

Content-Based Filtering

Recommendations based on the intrinsic attributes and features of items a user has interacted with previously.

Mechanism: If you read books tagged #SciFi and #AI, the engine recommends other items sharing those exact metadata attributes.

Design Thinking: Starting With People

Our design thinking session focused on a mindset that shaped the rest of the week: begin with the problem, not with a ready-made solution.

We learned and applied the five iterative stages of the human-centered design thinking process:

Stage 01 · Foundation Observe & Immerse

Empathize: Understand the Human Experience

Rather than assuming what users need, we immerse ourselves in their daily reality. We conduct interviews, observe behaviors, and uncover latent frustrations without imposing our own biases.

Guiding Question: What does the user feel, think, and struggle with when nobody is watching?
Stage 02 · Synthesis Problem Framing

Define: Pinpoint the Root Problem

Synthesize field observations to identify the core obstacle. A well-framed problem statement focuses on user needs rather than business objectives or pre-determined technical solutions.

Guiding Question: How might we solve the root cause rather than just addressing the surface symptom?
Stage 03 · Divergence Brainstorming

Ideate: Explore Unconstrained Possibilities

Generate a wide spectrum of ideas without immediate judgment. The goal is volume and diversity—pushing past conventional, obvious answers to uncover innovative approaches.

Guiding Question: What wild or non-traditional ways could transform this user journey entirely?
Stage 04 · Convergence Rapid Building

Prototype: Build to Learn

Transform ideas into tangible, low-cost prototypes. Whether a paper wireframe, mock flow, or simple simulation, prototypes allow teams to experiment quickly and fail cheaply.

Guiding Question: What is the fastest and cheapest artifact we can build to test our key hypothesis?
Stage 05 · Validation Feedback Loop

Test: Validate with Real Users

Put the prototype into real users' hands. Observe where they hesitate, listen to their candid reactions, and feed those insights back into empathy and refinement loops.

Guiding Question: Did this genuinely improve the user's friction point, and what should we iterate next?

The key lesson: the first idea is rarely the final answer. Better solutions come from curiosity, empathy, experimentation, and continuous iteration.

“Turns out the ‘obvious solution’ from minute one is rarely the right one. Rude, but fair.”

Applying Design Thinking: Eyewear Retail Case Study

We put the framework into practice through an eyewear retail case study. Working as a collaborative team, we examined the entire customer journey and looked beyond the physical product itself to identify challenges that customers face while choosing, purchasing, or wearing eyewear.

1. Customer Personas & Needs

Mapped distinct profiles—first-time prescription wearers, fashion-driven shoppers, and professionals with heavy screen fatigue.

  • Understanding emotional anxiety of changing appearance.
  • Varying priorities: budget, style vs optical accuracy.

2. Friction & Pain Points

Identified critical hurdles throughout the eyewear purchasing and selection cycle.

  • Frame fit uncertainty without prescription lenses fitted.
  • Opaque lens pricing and confusing coating add-ons.

3. Customer-Friendly Ideation

Discussed concepts to make the customer journey simpler, more intuitive, and confidence-building.

  • Face-shape AI matching and real-time AR try-on.
  • At-home trial kits and transparent tier breakdowns.

4. Practical Problem Connection

Tethered creative ideas back to viable, real-world user solutions rather than vanity tech gimmicks.

  • Ensuring accessibility across diverse age demographics.
  • Measurable reduction in post-purchase return friction.

The activity showed us that effective problem-solving is not about having the loudest idea in the room. It is about finding the idea that genuinely improves the user’s experience.

“Four eyes are better than two — pun fully intended.”
